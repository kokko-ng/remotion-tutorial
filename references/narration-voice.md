# Narration voice

The narration is a spoken-word script, adapted from the "technical audiobook"
craft. The listener may be watching loosely or not at all, so the audio must
teach on its own; the visuals confirm and anchor, they do not carry meaning
the narration skips.

## The mental test

Before writing any sentence, imagine the listener has their eyes closed,
hearing it once. They cannot re-read, so front-load the point. They cannot
scan headings, so orient them with verbal signposts ("now, two neighborhoods",
"here is the part that surprises people"). They will drift if it is dry, so
keep a warm, human, slightly conversational voice.

## The through-line

Pick one organizing sentence before writing anything, state it early, and
return to it at chapter boundaries. Examples that worked:

- Azure networking: "every cloud networking question is really about who gets
  to knock on whose door."
- System design: "the same three verbs keep coming back: store, move,
  remember."
- Mechanistic interpretability: "a machine we have not finished reading yet."

The through-line is also a design asset: recall scenes visualize it.

## The three-beat rhythm

For each concept, in order: what it is (one clear sentence), why it exists
(the problem it removes; the beat most explanations skip), then a concrete
five-second hook or analogy. One vivid physical analogy per hard idea, then
release it; never stack analogies. If the analogy breaks down in a way that
matters, say so briefly.

## Writing for the screen as well as the ear

- No lists, no code blocks, no "as you can see". Narrate ideas; the scene
  shows the diagram in sync via word-keyed reveals.
- Mention on-screen terms out loud. If a box labeled NSG appears, the words
  "network security group" must be spoken within a second of it. Pick reveal
  keywords that are distinctive words in the sentence (nouns beat articles).
- Keep sentences subtitle-friendly: they are chunked at roughly 46 characters.
  Very long clauses with no commas produce awkward subtitle breaks.
- Math and code are spoken in words ("multiply each input by its weight, add
  everything up") while the EquationBlock or CodePanel shows the notation.

## Structure and pacing

Open on the first real idea, never a meta introduction. Foundations before
frameworks. One or two synthesis passages near the end that walk a complete
example while naming every earlier concept. Close every chapter, and the
video, with a plain recap: restate the key terms in one sentence each, in the
video's own vocabulary, while a recap scene shows the terms with their short
definitions. No theatrical prompts. "Say it out loud", "close your eyes",
"your turn" and similar classroom gimmicks are banned; the user explicitly
rejected them. One genuine closing sentence is enough.

Vary paragraph and sentence length. A short, punchy line after several long
ones lands like a breath.

## Purpose tuning

- Interview prep: include clean out-loud phrasings and a rapid-fire question
  stretch near the end, each answer in the three-beat rhythm.
- Course revision: precise definitions and worked reasoning you can follow by
  ear; go easy on interview phrasing.
- Curiosity: lean hardest on intuition, history, and mental models.

## Substantive, not shallow

ELI5 is a tone, not a content level. Name the real terms, the genuine
trade-offs, the classic gotchas. After listening, the viewer should hold
their own in a real conversation. If it is all warm analogies and no
substance, the script has failed regardless of how pleasant it sounds.
